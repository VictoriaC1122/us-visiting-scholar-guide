Introduction

As I write, I have completed my J-1 interview and am preparing to leave for the United States. I’m Victoria, a PhD student in management. Applying for this visit has given me a closer understanding of the university reviews, supporting documents, and visa procedures that make a period of research abroad possible.

Information about the J-1 visa was relatively easy to find. The university procedures following the professor’s agreement took longer to understand: how the application would be reviewed, how English proficiency would be assessed, and what had to be completed before the DS-2019 could be issued. These details emerged at different stages. I often had to clarify one requirement before the next part of the application could proceed.

I have organized this account by application stage, keeping the sequence of events alongside the expenses and documents I can verify. The personal experience described here belongs to this particular application; university requirements and official rules need to be checked separately. The NSTC decision and arrival check-in are still pending, and I have marked those parts accordingly.

1. Contacting a professor through a shared research interest

Before sending the first email, I read the professor’s recent work alongside my own research and considered where a collaboration might be possible. I delayed sending the message for some time. The reply, when it came, was prompt: the professor was willing to host me.

The email itself was brief. I introduced my research, explained why I hoped to work with this professor, and outlined the proposed dates and collaboration, with my academic CV attached. Reading the professor’s work beforehand gave me something specific to say about the visit.

I was pleased to receive the reply. The department and college still needed to review the visit, however, and I submitted a research proposal as part of that process. About a month passed before I received the formal offer/appointment letter. In my application, the professor’s agreement and institutional approval involved distinct decisions and their own periods of waiting.

2. University paperwork and the DS-2019

The application passed through the department, college, and international office. Following the university’s checklist, I submitted my passport biographical page, CV, degree or enrollment verification, research summary, proof of funding, and evidence of English proficiency. The research arrangements, my eligibility, and the source of funding each needed supporting documentation.

The J-1 sponsor issues the DS-2019 after reviewing the documents and creating a SEVIS record; applicants cannot download and complete the form themselves. In my case, obtaining it depended on completing the preceding requirements and having those materials accepted.

Evidence of English proficiency and two IELTS exams

The host university required at least CEFR B1+. I took IELTS Academic; my result converted to a level above CEFR B2, meeting the university’s requirement.

One of those exams took place on May 8, 2026. The fee at the time was NT$8,300 per test, totaling NT$16,600 for both exams.

How I prepared for IELTS

I scheduled speaking and writing practice separately, using ChatGPT to support revision and organize notes on expression and writing.

  • Timed speaking practice focused on well-organized, complete responses.
  • For writing, I established the main argument before planning the paragraph structure.
  • Revision focused on precise wording, sentence structure, and connections between paragraphs.
After receiving the DS-2019

I checked my name, category, program dates, funding source, and site of activity. I would need the same form for the visa application, entry to the United States, and university check-in.

3. Preparing the NSTC funding application in parallel

While the university review was under way, I prepared an application for Taiwan’s National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) Graduate Students Study Abroad Program, commonly known as “Chien-Li-Ma.” This funding application follows a separate procedure from the DS-2019. Some materials could be used for both, including the language test result, the professor’s consent letter, and parts of the research proposal.

My application included proof of foreign-language ability, the overseas professor’s consent letter, eligibility documents, a recommendation letter, transcripts, a research proposal of no more than 20 pages, and at least one published work.

The research proposal required the most time. Once it was complete, I read it alongside the invitation letter and evidence of English proficiency, checking the purpose, dates, and research description across the documents. Each served a different purpose, but their accounts of the proposed visit needed to agree.

My application status Submitted; result pending

Results for the 2027 funding cycle (ROC year 116) are scheduled for November 30, 2026. The official notice allows for a delay if necessary. I have left the grant out of my budget while I wait for the decision.

Key rules for the 2027 funding cycle

EligibilityROC nationals with household registration in Taiwan who have completed at least one year of a domestic PhD program, are nominated by their university, and can finish the overseas research before graduation
FundingPartial support for 7–12 months, calculated by actual research days using NT$900,000 for one year as the benchmark
Application periodJune 1 to noon on July 31, 2026; results scheduled for November 30

After I completed my part in the system, my recommender uploaded the letter separately. My university then had to verify my eligibility and submit the institutional application. The official deadline was noon on July 31; universities usually set an earlier internal deadline. The host professor’s consent letter needed official letterhead, a date, and a signature. An email exchange alone was not accepted.

Reviewing the application against the assessment criteria

Before submission, I reviewed the application against the official scoring weights: 35% for background and development potential, 20% for language ability and the suitability of the host institution, 30% for the research proposal, and 15% for the project’s importance to Taiwan’s future development.

The university and NSTC use different English criteria

My host university required CEFR B1+. The 2027 NSTC call did not set a minimum IELTS score, but the same test result had to include listening, speaking, reading, and writing, and language ability was part of the review.

Previous recipients of this grant cannot apply again. During the funded period, recipients also cannot receive other Taiwanese government funding of a similar nature for overseas study or research, or for international conference participation. If I receive the funding, I will need to confirm whether I become subject to the two-year home-country physical presence requirement under section 212(e). If the answer is unclear, the U.S. Department of State’s Advisory Opinion process provides an official determination. The full rules are in the 2027 call for applications (ROC year 116) and the current program guidelines.

4. The J-1 visa and AIT interview

Once the DS-2019 had been issued and checked, I could begin the J-1 visa application. While completing the DS-160, I referred to the invitation documents and research materials to keep the information consistent. Those same documents later informed my preparation for the interview. The sequence was as follows:

  1. 01
    Pay the I-901 SEVIS fee

    I paid using the information from my sponsor and saved a copy of the receipt.

  2. 02
    Complete the DS-160

    I kept my passport, DS-2019, and program information beside me while filling in the form.

  3. 03
    Pay the visa fee and book the interview

    I paid the visa application fee, then booked through the AIT appointment system in use at the time.

  4. 04
    Prepare for the interview

    I practiced brief answers about the purpose of the visit, my research, funding, and plans to return.

  5. 05
    Receive and check the visa

    When my passport came back, I checked the spelling of my name in English and the visa details.

Preparing for the interview

My preparation centered on the purpose of the visit, the research, the source of funding, and my plans to return to Taiwan. Explaining the research took particular care: I practiced describing it in one or two sentences while remaining consistent with the DS-160, DS-2019, and invitation documents. I rehearsed brief, complete answers without memorizing a long script.

What I was asked

  • Why are you going to the United States, and what will you do there?
  • What is your current status and research topic?
  • How will the visit be funded? Do you have government funding?
  • Why will you return to Taiwan after the visit?
  • Have you made your social media accounts public?

At the interview

  • I answered each question briefly and left out details the officer had not asked for.
  • I explained that I was still waiting for the NSTC funding decision.
  • I wore formal clothes out of habit. AIT did not require formal dress.
Documents I brought to the interview

Concerned that I might need supporting evidence at the interview, I brought all the documents I thought could be relevant. The officer collected my passport, DS-2019, and photo; the remaining documents were available if needed.

Core documents: I used the official checklist and appointment notice to prepare my valid passport, DS-160 confirmation page, visa fee receipt if applicable, and DS-2019. Whether I needed a printed photo depended on the DS-160 photo upload result.

Backup materials: Previous passports if any, appointment confirmation, I-901 payment receipt, invitation/appointment documents, proof of funds issued in English, enrollment verification, CV, research proposal, and housing information.

The officer may ask for different documents in another interview. Follow the checklist in your own appointment notice.

Arrival time and security screening

My notice said I could be denied entry if I arrived more than 10 minutes early or late. I waited nearby until close to my appointment time. Electronic devices are not allowed in the consular waiting area, so I arranged storage in advance and left my laptop and other large electronics behind. The AIT security procedures ↗ list the current restrictions.

The interview ended sooner than I had expected, and my application was approved. The research and funding arrangements I had checked repeatedly during preparation were among the matters I needed to explain briefly in person.

For the official document list, use the U.S. Department of State J-1 guidance and your appointment notice. For the DS-160, you only need to bring the confirmation page; see the official FAQ. The questions above are the ones I personally received.

As of September 2026, F, M, and J applicants must follow official instructions to make their social media profiles public for online screening; applicants do not provide passwords. Before my interview, I also checked the AIT security procedures and the visa photo requirements again.

6. Before departure: research and living arrangements

With the visa approved, my attention turned to the research and living arrangements after arrival: what to discuss at the first meeting, which documents to prepare for check-in, and how to organize the move into my accommodation. I divided the remaining tasks into the three groups below.

Documents and check-in

  • Check the details on my passport, DS-2019, and visa
  • Confirm that my insurance meets J-1 and university requirements
  • Note the check-in deadline and contact person
  • Back up important documents

Research preparation

  • Revise my research slides for the first meeting
  • List my progress, unresolved problems, and questions
  • Schedule the first meeting after arrival
  • Plan the first few weeks of research

Living arrangements

  • Confirm housing and key collection
  • Look into transportation, phone service, and internet access
  • Set aside money for the deposit and initial expenses
  • Pack what I need for the first week and buy the rest locally

Housing and furniture

After securing accommodation, I confirmed the key collection time and inspection procedure before arranging furniture delivery. I plan to take photographs or video at move-in, following the management office’s instructions, so that the apartment’s condition can be checked against that record when I leave.

The contents of the furniture package also needed checking. A bed frame and mattress did not necessarily include pillows, a comforter, or sheets. Alongside the listed items and monthly rent, I confirmed the charges for delivery, installation, and pickup, as well as the return terms.

Insurance documents

For the university’s check-in documentation, I uploaded the coverage/visa letter and declaration page. The complete policy was not required for that submission, but I have retained it for reference. I will also provide an insurance card if the form requests one.

I checked the policy against 22 CFR § 62.14: at least US$100,000 in medical benefits per accident or illness, US$50,000 for medical evacuation to the home country, US$25,000 for repatriation of remains, and a deductible no higher than US$500 per accident or illness. I also checked the insurer’s rating against the regulation, matched the coverage dates to the latest DS-2019, and looked for any additional sponsor requirements.

These minimums come from 22 CFR § 62.14. The documents you actually upload depend on your sponsor’s instructions.

Documents in my carry-on

My carry-on checklist includes my passport, valid visa, original DS-2019, SEVIS fee receipt if applicable, invitation documents, housing information, and university contact details.

Booking the flight

I booked before my visa was issued. I read the change and cancellation terms first to make sure I could accept the risk if the visa did not arrive in time.

7. Arrival check-in: arrangements still to be completed

At the time of this update, I have not yet completed arrival check-in. The list below records the preparations set out in my sponsor’s instructions. I will add an account of the actual procedure after arrival.

  1. Download the I-94 and check the name, Class of Admission, and Admit Until Date.
  2. Complete sponsor check-in and orientation by the assigned deadlines.
  3. Activate the campus account, ID card, and access to the research space.
  4. Confirm the first meeting with the host professor. If the professor is away, record the alternate contact.
  5. Ask the RO/ARO about any J-1 status questions involving address changes, research sites, international travel, extensions, or employment.
If the arrival date changes

If my expected entry date changes, I will tell the international office and department, then ask the sponsor whether my appointment letter or DS-2019 needs updating. I will check the dates on any replacement documents.

According to BridgeUSA, for a program longer than 30 days, a participant with an entry record will have their SEVIS record changed to No Show if the sponsor does not validate it within 30 days after the program start date. The sponsor uses that window to process the record; participants still need to meet their check-in deadline.

The RO (Responsible Officer) and ARO (Alternate Responsible Officer) are the sponsor officials who handle J-1 status matters. See 22 CFR § 62.13 for the governing requirements and BridgeUSA for an explanation of SEVIS status changes.

Questions checked during the application

Does a professor’s agreement mean the application is finished?

In my case, the department, college, and sponsor still had to review and formally approve the visit after the professor agreed.

Can I download and complete the DS-2019 myself?

The designated sponsor creates the SEVIS record and issues the DS-2019. I provided the requested information and checked the form after receiving it.

How early can I enter the United States in J-1 status?

You may enter in J-1 status no earlier than 30 days before the program start date on the DS-2019.

Does ordinary travel insurance meet the J-1 requirements?

A travel policy may not meet the requirements. I checked the four minimums in 22 CFR § 62.14, the insurer’s eligibility, the coverage period, and my sponsor’s rules.

Is every J-1 exchange visitor subject to the two-year requirement under section 212(e)?

The requirement depends on the participant and program. If it is unclear whether it applies, ask the sponsor or request an Advisory Opinion from the U.S. Department of State.
