From the First Email to the J-1 Interview

Introduction

Hi, I’m Victoria, a PhD student in management. This is the U.S. visiting scholar application process I’ve completed so far.

When I started preparing, I found plenty of information about the J-1 visa. What was harder to find was what happened after a professor agreed to host me: the university’s internal approvals and how the DS-2019 was actually issued. That was also the part I spent the most time checking, so I wrote down the order, where things slowed down, and what I paid while it was still fresh.

This is my own experience, and every university may handle things differently. My NSTC grant application is still pending, and I have not completed the arrival process yet. I will update this page when there is more to add.

1. Put the research idea on one page

At first, I had no idea where to start. A visiting scholar application does not come with one standard application portal. I eventually made myself answer three questions: Where did I want to go? Who did I want to work with? What exactly did I want to finish during the visit? Once I had rough answers, I started organizing the materials.

I did not begin with a long research proposal. I first reduced everything to one page covering:

  • My research topic and current progress
  • Why I wanted to visit that particular institution
  • What I hoped to work on with the host professor
  • The proposed dates and expected outcomes
The one-page research summary

I originally made this page just to contact professors. It turned out to be useful again for the university paperwork and visa interview. My approach was to get the short version right first, then expand it whenever someone asked for a full proposal. That saved me from starting over every time.

2. Send the first email and find a host professor

Once I knew whom I wanted to contact, I read the professor’s recent work first to make sure there was a real fit between our research. I put off sending the email for a while, only to receive a positive reply quite quickly.

I kept the email short and followed this order:

What I included in the first email

  1. Who I was and what I was currently researching
  2. Why I was contacting this professor
  3. My proposed visit dates and how I imagined the collaboration
  4. My CV and one-page research summary

I was obviously happy when the professor agreed, but I quickly learned that “the professor said yes” and “the university formally approved the visit” are two separate things. The department and college still had to review the case, and I was asked for a full research proposal as well. It took about a month before I received the formal offer/appointment letter.

3. The university process starts after the professor says yes

Once the professor agreed, the actual university paperwork began. My documents went through the department, the college, and the international office. The package included my passport biographical page, CV, degree or enrollment verification, research summary, proof of funding, and English-language evidence.

One point that is easy to misunderstand: you do not download a DS-2019 and fill it out yourself. The J-1 sponsor reviews the materials, creates the SEVIS record, and then issues the form to you.

The English requirement: I took IELTS twice

I did not expect this part at all. The university required at least CEFR B1+. I first took its internal English assessment and did not pass, so I switched to IELTS Academic. I took the exam twice, and my final result corresponded to CEFR B2. That finally cleared the English requirement.

One of those exams was on May 8, 2026. The fee at the time was NT$8,300 per test, or NT$16,600 for both attempts. The DS-2019 review could not move forward until I submitted an acceptable result.

How I prepared for IELTS

English is not my strongest subject, so I practiced speaking and writing separately. I also used ChatGPT to identify problems and help me rewrite, then kept my own list of recurring mistakes.

  • For speaking, I timed myself on my phone and focused on finishing a complete response first.
  • For writing, I worked out the main point and paragraph structure before rewriting the weak parts.
  • Near the end, I mainly fixed literal translations from Chinese, overlong sentences, verb tense, and awkward transitions.
After receiving the DS-2019

I checked every field: name, category, program dates, funding source, and site of activity. The same form would later be used for the visa, entry to the United States, and university check-in.

4. Apply for the NSTC Graduate Students Study Abroad Program

While the university paperwork was moving, I also applied for Taiwan’s National Science and Technology Council (NSTC) Graduate Students Study Abroad Program, commonly known in Taiwan as the “Chien-Li-Ma” program. It is a separate application in a separate system and does not connect automatically to the DS-2019 process. The useful part was that I could reuse some materials, including my language test result, the host professor’s letter, and the research proposal. My application has been submitted, and the result is still pending.

My application included proof of foreign-language ability, the overseas professor’s consent letter, eligibility documents, a recommendation letter, transcripts, a research proposal of no more than 20 pages, and at least one published work.

The most time-consuming part was not the form. It was the research proposal. Before submitting, I compared the invitation letter, English-language evidence, and proposal side by side to make sure the purpose, dates, and research description all matched.

My application status Submitted; result pending

For the 2027 funding cycle (ROC year 116), results are scheduled for November 30, 2026, although the official notice says the announcement may be delayed if necessary. Until the decision is official, I am not counting the grant in my budget.

Key rules for the 2027 funding cycle

EligibilityROC nationals with household registration in Taiwan who have completed at least one year of a domestic PhD program, are nominated by their university, and can finish the overseas research before graduation
FundingPartial support for 7–12 months, calculated by actual research days using NT$900,000 for one year as the benchmark
Application periodJune 1 to noon on July 31, 2026; results scheduled for November 30

Submitting the online form was not the end. I completed my part in the system, my recommender uploaded the letter separately, and then the university had to verify my eligibility and submit the institutional application. The official deadline was noon on July 31, but universities usually set an earlier internal deadline. The host professor’s consent letter also needed official letterhead, a date, and a signature; an email exchange alone did not count.

How I checked the research proposal

Before submitting, I used the official scoring weights as a checklist: 35% for background and development potential, 20% for language ability and the suitability of the host institution, 30% for the research proposal, and 15% for the project’s importance to Taiwan’s future development.

The university and NSTC use different English criteria

My host university required CEFR B1+. The 2027 NSTC call did not set a minimum IELTS score, but the same test result had to include listening, speaking, reading, and writing, and language ability was part of the review.

Previous recipients of this grant cannot apply again. During the funded period, recipients also cannot receive other Taiwanese government funding of a similar nature for overseas study or research, or for international conference participation. If I receive the funding, I will need to confirm whether I become subject to the two-year home-country physical presence requirement under section 212(e). If the answer is unclear, the U.S. Department of State’s Advisory Opinion process provides an official determination. The full rules are in the 2027 call for applications (ROC year 116) and the current program guidelines.

5. The J-1 visa and AIT interview

Once the DS-2019 arrived and I had checked every field, I moved on to the J-1 visa. The part that took me the most time was making sure the DS-160, supporting documents, and interview answers all told the same story. This was my order:

  1. 01
    Pay the I-901 SEVIS fee

    I used the information from my sponsor and saved a separate copy of the receipt.

  2. 02
    Complete the DS-160

    I kept my passport, DS-2019, and program information beside me before starting the form.

  3. 03
    Pay the fee and schedule the interview

    After paying the visa application fee, I booked an appointment through the AIT system available at the time.

  4. 04
    Prepare for the interview

    I reduced my purpose, research, funding, and return plans to short answers.

  5. 05
    Receive and check the visa

    When my passport came back, I checked the English name and visa details again.

How I prepared for the AIT interview

I did not memorize a long script. I made sure I could explain four things clearly: why I was going, what I would do, where the money would come from, and why I would return to Taiwan afterward. I cross-checked those answers against the DS-160, DS-2019, and invitation documents, and practiced describing my research in one or two sentences.

What I was asked

  • Why are you going to the United States, and what will you do there?
  • What is your current status and research topic?
  • How will the visit be funded? Do you have government funding?
  • Why will you return to Taiwan after the visit?
  • Have you made your social media accounts public?

How I answered

  • The officer asked short questions, so I gave short answers instead of forcing in everything I had prepared.
  • The NSTC result was still pending, and I said exactly that.
  • I dressed formally because that is my habit, not because AIT required it.
Documents I brought to the interview

I was worried about forgetting something, so I brought a full folder. In the end, the officer at the window only asked for my passport, DS-2019, and photo.

Core documents: Following the official checklist and appointment notice, I prepared my valid passport, DS-160 confirmation page, visa fee receipt if applicable, and DS-2019. I followed the DS-160 upload result to decide whether to bring a printed photo.

Backup materials: Previous passports if any, appointment confirmation, I-901 payment receipt, invitation/appointment documents, proof of funds issued in English, enrollment verification, CV, research proposal, and housing information.

This was what happened in my interview. The appointment notice for your own case remains the final checklist.

Arrival time and security screening

My notice said that arriving more than 10 minutes early or late could mean being denied entry, so I waited nearby and approached closer to my appointment time. Electronic devices are not allowed in the consular waiting area. I arranged storage in advance and simply did not bring a laptop or large electronics. See the AIT security procedures ↗ for the current details.

The interview was much shorter than I expected, and the officer approved my visa application at the end.

For the official document list, use the U.S. Department of State J-1 guidance and your appointment notice. For the DS-160, you only need to bring the confirmation page; see the official FAQ. The questions above are the ones I personally received.

As of September 2026, F, M, and J applicants must follow official instructions to make their social media profiles public for online screening; applicants do not provide passwords. Before my interview, I also checked the AIT security procedures and the visa photo requirements again.

I ended up buying three kinds of insurance

The U.S. J-1 medical policy was required for the program. I bought the Taiwan overseas student policy on my own, and the renter’s policy was required for my housing. Together, the three policies cost NT$57,980.

Proof of funds is not an expense

The required amount on a proof-of-funds statement may look large, but it is not a payment. My sponsor calculated it as US$2,000 per month multiplied by the number of program months. I only had to show that I could cover the amount; I did not pay it to the university.

Not included in the total

I used 90,000 miles for my flight and paid a little over NT$3,000 in taxes. Since I only remember the approximate amount, I did not force it into the total. I will add rent, furniture, phone service, and living costs after I have the actual numbers.

The NSTC application itself did not have a separate fee. Because the result is still pending, I have not counted any possible funding.

7. What I handled after the visa was approved

Once the visa was done, I started working on documents, research, and day-to-day arrangements at the same time. I divided the checklist into three groups.

Documents and check-in

  • Check the passport, DS-2019, and visa details one by one
  • Confirm that the insurance meets both J-1 and university requirements
  • Record the check-in deadline and contact person
  • Back up important documents

Research preparation

  • Turn the research slides into something ready for an actual meeting
  • List current progress, sticking points, and questions to discuss
  • Schedule the first meeting after arrival
  • Plan the first few weeks of research

Living arrangements

  • Confirm housing and how to collect the keys
  • Research transportation, phone service, and internet after arrival
  • Set aside money for the deposit and initial expenses
  • Bring enough for the first week and buy the rest locally

Housing and furniture

After securing housing, I confirmed the key pickup time and move-in inspection process before arranging furniture delivery. On move-in day, I also planned to take photos or video according to the management office’s procedure so there would be a clear record at move-out.

When renting furniture, I learned that a package can include a bed frame and mattress without including a pillow, comforter, or sheets. In addition to the monthly price, I checked delivery, installation, pickup fees, and the return conditions.

Insurance documents

For my university check-in, I did not need to upload the full policy. I uploaded the coverage/visa letter and declaration page. If the form separately asked for an insurance card, I added that too. I kept the complete policy available in case anyone asked for it.

I checked the requirements in 22 CFR § 62.14 one by one: at least US$100,000 in medical benefits per accident or illness, US$50,000 for medical evacuation to the home country, US$25,000 for repatriation of remains, and a deductible no higher than US$500 per accident or illness. The insurer also had to meet the regulation’s rating requirements. I matched the coverage period against the latest DS-2019 and checked whether my sponsor imposed anything additional.

These minimums come from 22 CFR § 62.14. The documents you actually upload depend on your sponsor’s instructions.

Documents in my carry-on

I made a separate carry-on checklist: passport, valid visa, original DS-2019, SEVIS fee receipt if applicable, invitation documents, housing information, and university contact details.

Airfare change and cancellation rules

My visa had not been issued when I booked the flight, so I read the change and cancellation terms first and only booked once I could accept the risk.

8. There is another round of check-in after arrival

I have not completed this part yet. For now, the list below follows my sponsor’s instructions; I will add my actual experience after I arrive.

  1. Download the I-94 and check the name, Class of Admission, and Admit Until Date.
  2. Complete sponsor check-in and orientation by the assigned deadlines.
  3. Activate the campus account, ID card, and access to the research space.
  4. Confirm the first meeting with the host professor. If the professor is away, record the alternate contact.
  5. Direct any status-related questions about address changes, research sites, international travel, extensions, or employment to the RO/ARO.
If the arrival date changes

If my expected entry date changes, I will notify the international office and department first, then ask the sponsor whether the appointment letter or DS-2019 needs to be updated. If a new version is issued, I will check the dates again.

BridgeUSA also states that for a program longer than 30 days, if the participant has an entry record and the sponsor does not validate the SEVIS record within 30 days after the program start date, the record will change to No Show. Those 30 days are the sponsor’s processing window, not a grace period for delaying check-in.

The RO (Responsible Officer) and ARO (Alternate Responsible Officer) are the sponsor officials who handle J-1 status matters. See 22 CFR § 62.13 for the governing requirements and BridgeUSA for an explanation of SEVIS status changes.

Questions I kept checking during the application

Does a professor’s agreement mean the application is finished?

Not in my case. After the professor agreed, the department, college, and sponsor still had to complete their formal reviews.

Can I download and complete the DS-2019 myself?

No. A designated sponsor creates the SEVIS record and issues the DS-2019. My job was to provide the requested information and check every field after receiving it.

How early can I enter the United States in J-1 status?

You may enter in J-1 status no earlier than 30 days before the program start date on the DS-2019.

Does ordinary travel insurance meet the J-1 requirements?

Not necessarily. I separately checked the four minimums in 22 CFR § 62.14, the insurer eligibility requirement, the coverage period, and my sponsor’s own rules.

Is every J-1 exchange visitor subject to the two-year requirement under section 212(e)?

No. It depends on the participant and program. If the answer is unclear, ask the sponsor or request an Advisory Opinion from the U.S. Department of State.
